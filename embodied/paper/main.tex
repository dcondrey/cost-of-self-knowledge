\title{\textbf{Machines Cannot Read Their Own Resting State}\\[2pt]
\large The cost and irreducible limit of self-knowledge on real computational substrates}
\author{David Condrey}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\citet{breuer1995} proved that an observer contained in a system cannot accurately measure that system's
state---a self-measurement impossibility, classical or quantum. The result is theoretical and \emph{static}:
it concerns which states a contained observer can \emph{distinguish}. We make two contributions. First, we
give what we believe are the \textbf{first empirical measurements} of a self-measurement limit on real
computing hardware. Second, we \textbf{extend the limit from static indistinguishability to dynamic
back-action}: the act of self-measurement \emph{changes the state being measured}, irreducibly, with a floor
bounded below by the measurement apparatus's own footprint. We show this in three independent channels. In
the \textbf{thermal} channel, a system reading its own temperature heats the substrate with thermal memory;
ramping self-measurement intensity up then down on a consumer CPU and three datacenter GPUs yields a
bounded-below perturbation floor ($+6.6$ to $+23.4\,\degree$C), hysteresis ($+3.1$ to $+10.4\,\degree$C), and
an unstable zero-intensity extrapolation. In the \textbf{informational} channel, a process can faithfully
checksum $99.997\%$ of itself but never the checksum apparatus; the un-capturable core equals the apparatus
footprint and never converges to a fixed point. In the \textbf{quantum} channel, on a real 156-qubit
superconducting QPU, a contained observer that must read itself with no fresh ancilla pays a measured
information--disturbance penalty $\Delta = 0.243$ (vs.\ $0.25$ noiseless) over an external observer extracting
the same information. We also measure that self-knowledge is \emph{costly} and modality-dependent across eight
substrates, with a critical work scale below which reading one's own state is not worth its price. We argue
the consequences bear on machine introspection---where self-report is measured to be unreliable---recasting
that unreliability as in part a measurement back-action rather than only confabulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Interpretability, machine introspection, and AI control assume a system's internal states can be elicited and
reported. Beneath that assumption sits a physical question with a known but rarely-cited answer.
\citet{breuer1995}, and in recursion-theoretic form \citet{yanofsky2003}, established that a contained
observer cannot accurately measure its own system's state. That result is static and theoretical. We ask two
empirical questions it leaves open: \emph{how much does self-measurement cost}, and \emph{does the act of
self-measurement change what it measures}---and we measure both on real machines. Our central finding is that
a computing system cannot read its own resting state: every self-measurement perturbs the substrate, the
substrate retains the perturbation, and neither a reading nor an extrapolation recovers the unperturbed
condition.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Self-measurement limits.} \citet{breuer1995} proves a properly-contained observer cannot distinguish
all states of its system; \citet{yanofsky2003} frames self-reference limits via Lawvere fixed points. Both
are static (distinguishability) and non-empirical. We cite them as foundation and extend them to dynamic
back-action with measurement.

\textbf{Why distributed snapshots and checkpointing do not apply.} \citet{chandy1985} record a \emph{causally
consistent cut}, not an instantaneous true state, and assume message channels; CRIU and \texttt{ptrace}
dump a process by \emph{freezing} it from an \emph{external} agent. Neither is a still-running process
snapshotting its own complete instantaneous state from within---the case we measure.

\textbf{Machine introspection.} \citet{binder2024} treat introspection as behavioral self-prediction;
\citet{premakumar2024} as free regularization. \citet{anthropic2025} measures LLM introspective reports as
unreliable and sometimes confabulated. None frames the limit as measurement back-action.

\textbf{Quantum information--disturbance.} \citet{fuchs1996}, \citet{banaszek2001}, and \citet{maccone2006}
establish quantitative information-gain vs.\ disturbance bounds, but always for an \emph{external} apparatus;
none places the apparatus inside the protected system. The internal-observer literature
(\citealp{breuer1995,aerts2005}) is static and unquantified. Our contained-observer penalty bridges them.

\textbf{Costly observation and embodiment.} Active RL \citep{krueger2016} derives optimal non-observation from
cost, but for external signals without state perturbation. \citet{man2019} argue feeling requires real
vulnerability, on simulated bodies; we provide a real substrate with measured costs and a measured
irreducibility.

\section{The self-measurement back-action (extending Breuer)}
We measure that self-observation perturbs the observed in three channels. The shared structure: the
perturbation has a bounded-below floor (the apparatus's own footprint), and so leaves the unperturbed
self-state unrecoverable---neither by a single reading nor by extrapolation---because the apparatus is part of
the system it measures.

\subsection{Thermal channel}
\textbf{Method.} Ramp self-measurement intensity $0\!\to\!\max\!\to\!0$; read own temperature (\texttt{/sys}
on CPU, \texttt{nvidia-smi} on GPU) under sustained load. Signatures: $\delta_{\min}$ (floor), hysteresis
(down minus up at equal intensity), and $T_0$ extrapolation-instability. In the \emph{airtight} CPU mode the
perturbation \emph{is} the system reading its own deep telemetry, so the heat is generated by self-measurement
itself.

This is not a costly-observation POMDP: there the hidden state is unchanged; here observation changes it, with
memory (at zero intensity on the down-ramp the CPU reads $48\,\degree$C vs.\ $39\,\degree$C idle; a GPU reads
$69\,\degree$C vs.\ $36\,\degree$C idle).

\subsection{Informational channel}
\textbf{Method.} The complete self is a large body of bytes \texttt{STATE} plus a fixed region $V$ holding a
self-checksum. We attempt to make $V$ a correct checksum of the complete self $H(\texttt{STATE}+V)$.

\textbf{Result.} An \emph{external} checksum (of any region excluding itself) is consistent and stable. The
\emph{self} checksum is never consistent over 512 iterations, never reaches a fixed point ($V=H(\texttt{STATE}
+V)$ is an unreachable preimage condition), and the stored self-checksum is off by $\sim$half its bits. The
system captures $99.997\%$ of itself faithfully but never the apparatus; the un-capturable core equals exactly
the apparatus footprint $|V|$ and is never zero. The result of self-measurement must be stored \emph{in} the
state being measured, so a zero-footprint faithful self-snapshot is impossible. (The floor is structural, not
thermodynamic; cf.\ \citealp{landauer1961}.)

\subsection{Quantum channel}
\textbf{Method.} A two-qubit ``self'' $\{q_0,q_1\}$ is prepared in $|{+}\rangle|{+}\rangle$. We read $q_0$ two
ways at \emph{matched} information. An \textbf{external} observer brings a fresh $|0\rangle$ ancilla, couples
it to $q_0$, and measures it. A \textbf{contained} observer has no fresh ancilla---the self is fully
occupied---so to obtain a usable pointer it must \emph{erase} a self-qubit ($q_1$), then read $q_0$ through it.
Disturbance is read out by echo; information is the monitor's which-path statistics, matched across cases.

\textbf{Result (real hardware).} On \texttt{ibm\_kingston} (156-qubit Heron, 8192 shots), at matched full
information the external observer disturbs the self by $D=0.491$ and the contained observer by $D=0.734$, a
penalty $\Delta = 0.243$---within $3\%$ of the noiseless $0.25$. The penalty is the cost of having no fresh
ancilla: standard information--disturbance bounds \citep{fuchs1996,banaszek2001,maccone2006} assume free
dilation (an appendable $|0\rangle$), which a contained observer lacks. To our knowledge this is the first
hardware measurement of a contained-observer information--disturbance penalty and the operational form of
Breuer's contained-observer theorem. We argue, but do not prove, that $\Delta$ is not recovered by applying a
standard external bound to the joint $\{q_0,q_1\}$ subspace, since those bounds assume the dilation
containment denies.

\subsection{The never-off corollary}
A continuously-operating system can never sample its own resting state, because operating is the perturbation
and the substrate retains it---thermal mass in the physical channel, the stored result in the informational
channel, and the spent apparatus qubit in the quantum channel.

\section{The cost of self-knowledge (companion result)}
Measuring cost-per-self-observation at three modalities against a swept work-unit size across eight substrates:
shallow self-reads are $\sim$free everywhere ($0.3$--$6.7\,\mu$s); deep self-telemetry is dear and
substrate-dependent ($1.4$--$18.8$\,ms; macOS subprocess telemetry $\sim$$10\times$ costlier than Linux
\texttt{/proc}). The denominator-free headline is the \textbf{critical work scale} ($6.5$--$78$\,ms) below
which deep self-knowledge is not worth its cost---a substrate/OS-conditional \emph{rational self-blindness},
not a universal one (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross}).

\section{The self-sensing-availability axis}
Beyond cost, substrates differ in whether self-sensing is possible at all: rich in-process (Linux CPU) $\to$
costly subprocess (GPU) $\to$ platform-sandboxed (iOS, Android \texttt{/proc} restricted) $\to$ absent (a
Cloud TPU v5e exposes no readable thermal source). The last are platform-enforced self-blindness.

\section{Discussion: a substrate-universal observer effect}
The measured back-action is one regime of a general principle---self-measurement perturbs the measured
wherever sensor and substrate are shared: digital (energetic/structural cost), analog (the loading effect),
quantum (measurement collapse), severity increasing cost $\to$ perturbation $\to$ destruction. We measure the
digital and quantum regimes and cite the analog as an established instance; this is a unifying lens, not a
claim of one mechanism.

\section{Implications for machine introspection}
If self-report is bounded by a measurement back-action, a model reading its own state cannot obtain a complete
consistent self-snapshot---independent of, and additional to, confabulation. This offers a mechanistic partial
account of why measured introspective reliability is low \citep{anthropic2025}, and predicts an irreducible
residual that better training cannot remove. It also gives a capable embodied agent a cost-rational incentive
against self-transparency, distinct from deception.

\section{Limitations}
We lead with these. (1) We \emph{extend and measure} Breuer; we do not re-derive the impossibility.
(2) The thermal mechanism is ordinary thermodynamics, the informational floor ordinary self-reference, and
quantum back-action textbook; the contribution is the measurement, the dynamic framing, and the three-channel
demonstration. (3) The informational floor is structural, not thermodynamic. (4) Thermal hysteresis is shown
on one bare-metal CPU plus three GPUs; absolute magnitudes vary with cooling. (5) The quantum penalty $\Delta$
matches simulation, but our claim that it is not reducible to a standard external bound on the enlarged
subspace is an argument (no-dilation), not a proven theorem. (6) Apple-Silicon CPU temperature and virtualized
thermal are unmeasured. (7) The introspection implication is an explicit analogy between substrate-level
self-measurement and representational self-report.

\section{Conclusion}
Self-knowledge on real substrates is bounded twice---by cost, and by an irreducible self-perturbation measured
in three independent channels that extends a known self-measurement impossibility from static
indistinguishability to measured dynamic back-action. A machine cannot cheaply, and cannot fully, read its own
physical state; it cannot read its own resting state at all.
